Parametric EQ insert · mockup for review · 196 px rack

An eight-band mixing EQ for part strips, send buses and the master. Each band is a filter you place on the curve: cut the lows, notch a ringing note, lift the presence, roll off the top. The card below works. Drag the numbered points, scroll over one to change its width, and double-click an empty spot to add a band there. It makes no sound, and the spectrum behind the curve is a stand-in for a pad part.

How you use it

  • Drag a point left and right for frequency. For a bell or a shelf, up and down is gain. For a cut, up and down is resonance (Q). A notch only moves sideways.
  • Scroll over a point, or hold Alt while dragging, to change Q. Shift slows any drag for fine moves.
  • Double-click an empty spot to turn on the first free band as a bell at that frequency and gain. Double-click a point to put its gain back to 0 dB.
  • The band row above the curve selects a band. Its small square turns the band on or off. The panel on the right always edits the selected band.
  • Listen on drag: while you hold a point, you hear only what that band covers, as a band-pass at its frequency and Q, so you can find a ringing note before you cut it. The sound returns to normal when you let go. This is never saved.
  • Keys when the curve has focus: 1–8 select a band, arrows move it, Alt+↑/↓ change Q, Shift takes finer steps.
  • ±12 / ±24 zooms the curve only. The spectrum, the view range and which band is selected are session view state, like a rack page, and are never saved.

A new EQ is flat, with bands 2–7 on at 0 dB so there is a point to grab everywhere: 1 Low cut 30 Hz (off), 2 Low shelf 100 Hz, 3 Bell 250 Hz, 4 Bell 800 Hz, 5 Bell 2.5 kHz, 6 Bell 6 kHz, 7 High shelf 10 kHz, 8 High cut 18 kHz (off). A flat EQ passes the signal through untouched and costs almost nothing.

Sound engine

One AudioWorklet per EQ, stereo, zero latency, no oversampling. An EQ is linear, so it creates no new frequencies and has nothing to alias. Oversampling would only fix the curve's shape near the top, and the filter design below fixes that directly.

  • Accurate at the top. The usual textbook biquads squeeze a bell or a cut near 20 kHz: a 12 kHz bell comes out narrower and lopsided at 48 kHz. Bells and cuts use matched second-order sections instead (Vicanek, Matched Second Order Digital Filters, 2016), which keep the analog shape up to Nyquist. Shelves and the notch keep the prewarped bilinear form unless the spike shows audible error. The first ticket measures both against the analog curve before either is chosen.
  • The curve you see is the filter you hear. One pure coefficient module in inserts/ serves both the worklet and the console's drawing, as Advanced Drive's filter plots already do. The console draws the digital response from the real coefficients, not an ideal.
  • Smooth under automation. Frequency, gain and Q glide on a short one-pole (log for frequency and Q). Coefficients refresh every 16 samples while a value moves and not at all once it settles. Type, slope and on/off changes crossfade the band against its own input over about 10 ms.
  • Clean numbers. Transposed direct form II in 64-bit state, so a 20 Hz 48 dB cut stays quiet. Tiny state values are flushed so a silent tail never goes denormal.
  • Cheap on many strips. Off and flat bands are skipped. A fully flat EQ copies input to output. Silent input with settled state outputs silence without filtering. The cost of 16 EQs on 16 parts is measured on the load meter, not estimated.
  • Cuts: 12 dB is one section at the band's Q. 24 and 48 dB are Butterworth cascades whose last section takes the band's resonance, so Q 0.71 is flat and higher Q adds a bump at the corner.

How it gets built

  1. Engine: the eq insert kind. Spec, normaliser, constants, the coefficient and response module, the worklet, the registry entry and the index exports (the response function for drawing). Tests: the null test (flat = input, bit for bit), the swept response against the drawn curve, the matched-vs-bilinear error table in docs/research/, a click test while sweeping, and a 16-instance load reading. Class reviewed (DSP). Worker: worker.
  2. Console: the EQ card. This layout in the rack, the curve and its gestures, the band row and panel, the add picker's new EQ group listed first. The gestures' state lives in a pure model with its own tests. Class reviewed (UI). Needs 1.
  3. Spectrum and Listen. The engine gains an opt-in analyser tap on the EQ's output (read like the compressor's gain reduction, active only while the card is on screen) and a live-only listen band. Class reviewed. Needs 1. Can run beside 2 and join it at the end.

The song format gains one kind, { kind: 'eq', enabled, scale, output, bands: [8 × { on, type, slope, freq, gain, q }] }. It is additive, so ARRANGEMENT_VERSION stays where it is. A decision record lands with ticket 1.

Questions for tacowars

Eight bands? Enough for a cut at each end plus six moves. Recommend 8

Mid/side per band? A per-band Stereo / Mid / Side choice would let you, say, cut side lows or lift mid presence. It costs one field and an M/S encode only when a band uses it. Recommend later, as an additive field

Gain range. ±24 dB leaves room for deep cuts that aren't quite a notch; ±15 dB keeps knob moves finer. Recommend ±24, view at ±12

Spectrum and Listen in the first release? Both help most when you're hunting a note to notch. They are ticket 3 and can ship a little after the EQ itself. Recommend yes, as ticket 3

Q that tightens with gain? Some EQs narrow a bell as you push it harder, like analog desks. It sounds musical, but the curve no longer follows the knob exactly. Recommend leaving it out

Name. "Parametric EQ" on the rail, kind eq, in a new "EQ" group at the top of the add picker. Recommend as shown
